@ARTICLE Classifying Matter
# Pure substances
Fixed composition, cannot be separated
by physical means.
- Element: one kind of atom. Cannot be
broken down chemically.
- Compound: two or more elements
chemically bonded in a fixed ratio. Can
be broken down only chemically.

# Mixtures
Variable composition, separated by
physical means.
- Homogeneous (solution): uniform
throughout, one visible phase. Air,
salt water, brass.
- Heterogeneous: visibly different
regions. Sand and water, salad, granite.

# The test
If you can separate it by filtering,
evaporating or a magnet, it is a
mixture. If you need a chemical reaction,
it is a compound.

# Law of definite proportions
A compound always contains the same
elements in the same mass ratio. Water
is always 11.2% H and 88.8% O by mass.

@ARTICLE Physical and Chemical Change
# Physical properties
Observed without changing the identity
of the substance.
  color, odor, density, melting point,
  boiling point, solubility, hardness,
  conductivity, malleability

# Chemical properties
Describe how a substance reacts.
  flammability, reactivity with acid,
  toxicity, ability to rust

# Physical change
Identity is unchanged. Phase changes,
dissolving, crushing, cutting.

# Chemical change
New substances form. Signs to look for:
- gas produced (bubbling)
- precipitate forms
- unexpected color change
- heat or light given off or absorbed
- change is hard to reverse

# Careful
Dissolving salt is physical; the salt is
recoverable by evaporation. Boiling
water is physical, even though it looks
dramatic.

@ARTICLE Intensive and Extensive
# Intensive properties
Do not depend on how much you have.
  density, temperature, color, melting
  point, boiling point, concentration
Useful for identifying a substance.

# Extensive properties
Depend on the amount present.
  mass, volume, length, total energy,
  moles

# The test
Cut the sample in half. Anything that
changes is extensive.

# Why it matters
Density identifies a substance because
it is intensive: a gold ring and a gold
bar have the same density.

@ARTICLE Measurement and SI Units
# Base units
  mass          kilogram   kg
  length        meter      m
  time          second     s
  temperature   kelvin     K
  amount        mole       mol
  current       ampere     A

# Common derived units
  volume        L, mL, cm^3
  density       g/mL or g/cm^3
  energy        joule J, calorie cal
  pressure      atm, kPa, mmHg, torr
  concentration mol/L (M)

# Prefixes
  nano   n   1e-9
  micro  u   1e-6
  milli  m   1e-3
  centi  c   1e-2
  kilo   k   1e3
  mega   M   1e6

# Useful equalities
  1 mL = 1 cm^3
  1 L = 1000 mL = 1000 cm^3
  1 kg = 1000 g
  1 m = 100 cm = 1000 mm

# Temperature
  K = C + 273.15
  C = (5/9)(F - 32)
Kelvin is required in all gas law and
thermodynamics calculations.

@ARTICLE Significant Figures
# Counting rules
1. All nonzero digits count.
2. Zeros BETWEEN nonzero digits count.
3. Leading zeros never count.
4. Trailing zeros count only if there is
a decimal point.

# Examples
  0.00340    3 sig figs
  1200       2 sig figs
  1200.      4 sig figs
  1.200e3    4 sig figs
  100.0      4 sig figs

# Multiplying and dividing
The answer takes the FEWEST sig figs of
any factor.
  4.56 * 1.4 = 6.4

# Adding and subtracting
The answer takes the fewest DECIMAL
PLACES.
  12.11 + 18.0 = 30.1

# Exact numbers
Counted objects and defined conversions
(1 in = 2.54 cm) have infinite sig figs
and never limit the answer.

# Rounding
Round only at the very end. Rounding in
the middle of a multi-step problem
introduces error.

@ARTICLE Dimensional Analysis
The single most useful technique in
chemistry: multiply by conversion
factors until the unwanted units cancel.

# Setup
  given * (want/have) = answer
Put the unit you want to cancel on the
bottom.

# Example
Convert 25.0 g of water to moles:
  25.0 g * (1 mol/18.02 g) = 1.39 mol

# Multi-step
  g -> mol -> particles
  g * (1 mol/molar mass)
    * (6.022e23/1 mol)

# Percent as a factor
A 5.0% solution by mass gives
  (5.0 g solute/100 g solution)

# Checking
If the units of your answer are wrong,
the setup is wrong. Do the unit algebra
first, then reach for the calculator.

@ARTICLE Density
  d = m/V
  water = 1.00 g/mL at 4 C

# Rearranged
  m = d*V
  V = m/d

# Units
Solids and liquids in g/mL or g/cm^3;
gases in g/L, because gases are about a
thousand times less dense.

# Floating
An object floats in a liquid if its
density is lower. Ice (0.92 g/mL) floats
on water.

# Water displacement
Volume of an irregular solid:
  V = V_final - V_initial

# Some densities (g/mL)
