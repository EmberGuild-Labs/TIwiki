@CATEGORY Organic Chemistry

@ARTICLE Hydrocarbons
Compounds of carbon and hydrogen only.
Carbon forms 4 bonds, which is what makes
the enormous variety possible.

# Alkanes: single bonds, saturated
  CnH(2n+2)
  methane  CH4     pentane  C5H12
  ethane   C2H6    hexane   C6H14
  propane  C3H8    heptane  C7H16
  butane   C4H10   octane   C8H18
Suffix -ane. Relatively unreactive; they
burn and undergo substitution.

# Alkenes: at least one C=C
  CnH2n
  ethene C2H4, propene C3H6
Suffix -ene. Unsaturated and much more
reactive; they undergo addition.

# Alkynes: at least one triple bond
  CnH(2n-2)
  ethyne C2H2 (acetylene)
Suffix -yne.

# Aromatics
Benzene C6H6 is a flat ring with
delocalized electrons, drawn as a hexagon
with a circle. Unusually stable, so it
substitutes rather than adds.

# Saturated vs unsaturated
Saturated means all single bonds and the
maximum hydrogen. Unsaturated has double
or triple bonds. Bromine water is
decolorized by unsaturated compounds,
which is the standard test.

@ARTICLE Naming Organic Compounds
# Steps
1. Find the longest continuous carbon
chain; that gives the root name.
2. Number from the end that gives the
substituents the lowest numbers.
3. Name and number each branch.
4. Use di-, tri-, tetra- for repeats.
5. List the branches alphabetically,
ignoring the multiplying prefixes.

# Root by carbon count
  1 meth   5 pent   8 oct
  2 eth    6 hex    9 non
  3 prop   7 hept  10 dec
  4 but

# Branch names
  CH3-  methyl
  C2H5- ethyl
  C3H7- propyl

# Example
  CH3-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH3
  2-methylbutane

# Double bond position
Number so the double bond gets the
lowest possible number:
  but-2-ene or 2-butene

# Isomers
Same molecular formula, different
structure.
- Structural: different connectivity
(butane and 2-methylpropane)
- Geometric (cis/trans): different
arrangement across a rigid double bond
- Optical: mirror images that cannot be
superimposed

@ARTICLE Functional Groups
The reactive part of a molecule; it
determines the chemistry far more than
the carbon skeleton does.

# The main groups
  alcohol      -OH        -ol
  aldehyde     -CHO       -al
  ketone       -CO-       -one
  carboxylic   -COOH      -oic acid
  ester        -COO-      -oate
  ether        -O-        ether
  amine        -NH2       -amine
  amide        -CONH2     -amide
  halide       -X         halo-

# Alcohols
  methanol CH3OH, ethanol C2H5OH
Hydrogen bond, so they boil high and
dissolve in water.

# Carboxylic acids
  ethanoic (acetic) acid CH3COOH
Weak acids, sour, hydrogen bonded.

# Esters
Made from an acid plus an alcohol,
releasing water. Sweet smelling; they
give fruits their scents.

# Amines
Weak bases, derived from ammonia, and
they smell of fish.

# Why boiling points differ
  alkane < ether < aldehyde/ketone
  < alcohol < carboxylic acid
Rising with the strength of the
intermolecular forces.

@ARTICLE Organic Reactions
# Combustion
  hydrocarbon + O2 -> CO2 + H2O
Incomplete combustion gives CO and soot.

# Substitution
An atom replaces another. Typical of
alkanes, and it needs UV light.
  CH4 + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + HCl

# Addition
Something adds across a double or triple
bond, which opens. Typical of alkenes.
  C2H4 + Br2 -> C2H4Br2
  C2H4 + H2 -> C2H6  (hydrogenation)

# Elimination
The reverse of addition; a small molecule
leaves and a double bond forms.

# Esterification
  acid + alcohol <-> ester + water
Reversible, and acid catalyzed.

# Polymerization
Many monomers join into a chain.
- Addition: alkene monomers open their
double bonds. Polyethene, PVC,
polystyrene.
- Condensation: monomers join and
release a small molecule, usually water.
Nylon, polyester, and proteins from
amino acids.

# Biological polymers
  starch and cellulose from glucose
  proteins from amino acids
  DNA from nucleotides
