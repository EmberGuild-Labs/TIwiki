@CATEGORY Kinetics and Equilibrium

@ARTICLE Reaction Rates
  rate = change in concentration/time
Units usually M/s.

# From a balanced equation
Rates relate through the coefficients:
  aA + bB -> cC
  -(1/a)d[A]/dt = (1/c)d[C]/dt
Reactants are consumed, so their rate is
written with a minus sign.

# Collision theory
A reaction needs collisions that are
1. energetic enough to reach the
activation energy, and
2. correctly oriented.
Most collisions fail on one or both
counts.

# Activation energy Ea
The energy barrier between reactants and
products. A higher barrier means a slower
reaction.

# Energy diagrams
Reactants on the left, products on the
right, the peak is the transition state.
  Ea = peak - reactants
  dH = products - reactants
Exothermic diagrams end lower than they
start.

# Arrhenius equation
  k = A*e^(-Ea/(RT))
Raising the temperature increases k
sharply because it puts far more
molecules above Ea.

@ARTICLE Factors Affecting Rate
# Temperature
The biggest effect. A rough rule is that
a 10 C rise roughly doubles the rate.
More collisions, and far more of them
above Ea.

# Concentration
More particles per volume means more
collisions per second. For gases,
increasing the pressure does the same.

# Surface area
Only for heterogeneous reactions. Powder
reacts much faster than a lump, which is
why grain dust can explode.

# Catalyst
Provides a different pathway with a
LOWER activation energy. It is not
consumed and does not change dH, dG or
the position of equilibrium; it just
gets there faster, and speeds the forward
and reverse reactions equally.

# Nature of the reactants
Ionic reactions in solution are nearly
instant; reactions that break covalent
bonds are slower.

# Enzymes
Biological catalysts, highly specific,
and sensitive to temperature and pH.

@ARTICLE Rate Laws
  rate = k[A]^m[B]^n
m and n are the ORDERS. They must be
found experimentally and are NOT the
coefficients from the equation.

# Overall order
  m + n

# Reading experimental data
Compare two trials where only one
concentration changes.
- Double [A], rate unchanged: order 0
- Double [A], rate doubles: order 1
- Double [A], rate quadruples: order 2

# Integrated rate laws
  zero order:  [A] = -kt + [A]0
    straight line on [A] vs t
  first order: ln[A] = -kt + ln[A]0
    straight line on ln[A] vs t
  second order: 1/[A] = kt + 1/[A]0
    straight line on 1/[A] vs t

# Half-life
  first order: t = 0.693/k
Only for first order is the half-life
independent of concentration, which is
why radioactive decay works that way.

# Units of k
  zero order:   M/s
  first order:  1/s
  second order: 1/(M*s)

# Mechanisms
The slowest elementary step is the rate
determining step, and its molecularity
does set the rate law.

@ARTICLE Equilibrium and Keq
Equilibrium is reached when the forward
and reverse rates are EQUAL. The
concentrations stop changing but both
reactions continue: it is dynamic, not
stopped.

# Equilibrium constant
For aA + bB <-> cC + dD
  K = [C]^c[D]^d / ([A]^a[B]^b)
Products over reactants, each raised to
its coefficient.

# What to leave out
Pure solids and pure liquids are omitted
because their concentrations do not
change. Solvent water in dilute solution
is omitted too.

# Reading K
  K >> 1  products favored
  K << 1  reactants favored
  K near 1  both significant

# Kp and Kc
  Kp = Kc(RT)^dn
dn is the change in moles of gas.

# Manipulating K
- Reverse the reaction: K becomes 1/K
- Multiply coefficients by n: K^n
- Add reactions: multiply the K values

# ICE tables
Initial, Change, Equilibrium. Put in the
initial values, express the change with
x scaled by the coefficients, then solve.
If K is very small, assume x is
negligible against the initial value and
check that it is under 5%.

@ARTICLE Le Chatelier's Principle
A system at equilibrium responds to a
stress by shifting to partly relieve it.

# Concentration
Add a substance and the system shifts
AWAY from it. Remove one and it shifts
TOWARD it. Removing a product as it
forms drives a reaction forward.

# Pressure and volume
Only affects gases. Increasing the
pressure (smaller volume) shifts toward
the side with FEWER moles of gas. If the
gas moles are equal on both sides, there
is no shift.

# Adding an inert gas
At constant volume, no effect: the
partial pressures do not change.

# Temperature
The only stress that changes K itself.
Treat heat as a reactant or product.
- Exothermic: heat is a product, so
raising T shifts LEFT and lowers K
- Endothermic: raising T shifts RIGHT
and raises K

# Catalyst
No shift at all. It only shortens the
time to reach equilibrium.

# Reaction quotient Q
Same expression as K, using current
concentrations.
  Q < K  shifts right (toward products)
  Q > K  shifts left
  Q = K  at equilibrium

@ARTICLE Solubility Product
For a slightly soluble salt:
  AgCl(s) <-> Ag+ + Cl-
  Ksp = [Ag+][Cl-]
The solid is not in the expression.

# General form
  AxBy <-> xA + yB
  Ksp = [A]^x[B]^y

# Molar solubility s
For AgCl: [Ag+] = [Cl-] = s, so
  Ksp = s^2 and s = sqrt(Ksp)
For CaF2: [Ca2+] = s, [F-] = 2s, so
  Ksp = s(2s)^2 = 4s^3

# Comparing salts
You can only compare Ksp values directly
for salts with the same ion ratio.

# Common ion effect
Adding an ion already in the equilibrium
pushes it left and LOWERS the solubility.
AgCl is far less soluble in NaCl solution
than in pure water.

# Predicting a precipitate
Compute Q with the mixed concentrations.
  Q > Ksp  precipitate forms
  Q = Ksp  saturated
  Q < Ksp  no precipitate
