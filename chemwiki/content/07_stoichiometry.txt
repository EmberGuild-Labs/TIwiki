@CATEGORY Stoichiometry

@ARTICLE The Mole
  1 mol = 6.022e23 particles
  N_A = 6.022e23 /mol
Avogadro's number. A mole is just a
count, like a dozen, chosen so that
atomic mass units scale up to grams.

# The three faces of a mole
  1 mol = 6.022e23 particles
  1 mol = molar mass in grams
  1 mol gas = 22.4 L at STP

# Molar mass
Add the atomic masses from the periodic
table, in g/mol.
  H2O = 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol
  Ca(NO3)2 = 40.08 + 2(14.01)
           + 6(16.00) = 164.10 g/mol

# Conversions
  mol = mass/molar mass
  mass = mol * molar mass
  particles = mol * 6.022e23

# Particles vs atoms
One mole of H2O contains 6.022e23
MOLECULES but 3 times that many atoms.
Read the question carefully.

@ARTICLE Mole Conversions
Everything routes through moles. Draw
the map before calculating.

  mass <-> MOLES <-> particles
            ^
            |
      volume of gas (22.4 L at STP)
      or volume of solution (M = mol/L)

# The four bridges
  grams to moles:  divide by molar mass
  moles to grams:  multiply
  moles to particles: * 6.022e23
  moles of gas to L at STP: * 22.4

# Between two substances
Use the coefficients from the BALANCED
equation as a mole ratio. This is the
only step that crosses from one
substance to another.

# Example
How many grams of H2O from 10.0 g CH4?
  CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O
  10.0 g CH4 * (1 mol/16.04 g)
    = 0.623 mol CH4
  * (2 mol H2O/1 mol CH4)
    = 1.247 mol H2O
  * (18.02 g/mol) = 22.5 g H2O

# The universal recipe
grams of A -> moles of A -> moles of B
-> grams of B

@ARTICLE Limiting Reactant
The reactant that runs out first sets
how much product you can make.

# Method 1: make the product
Convert EACH reactant to moles of the
same product. The smaller answer is
correct, and that reactant is limiting.

# Method 2: compare ratios
Divide each reactant's moles by its
coefficient. The smallest value is
limiting.

# Excess reactant left over
  used = moles limiting * ratio
  left = initial - used
Convert back to grams if asked.

# Example
2.0 mol H2 with 2.0 mol O2:
  2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O
  H2: 2.0/2 = 1.0
  O2: 2.0/1 = 2.0
H2 is limiting, giving 2.0 mol H2O and
leaving 1.0 mol O2 unused.

# Warning
You cannot tell by mass alone which
runs out. Always convert to moles first.

@ARTICLE Percent Yield
  % yield = (actual/theoretical) * 100

# Theoretical yield
The maximum from the stoichiometry,
starting from the limiting reactant.

# Actual yield
What was really collected in the lab.
Always measured, never calculated.

# Why yields fall short
- The reaction does not go to completion
- Side reactions consume reactants
- Product is lost transferring or
filtering
- Impure reactants

# Over 100%
Not physically possible. It means the
product is still wet or contaminated.

# Percent error
  % error = |actual - accepted|
            /accepted * 100

@ARTICLE Percent Composition
  % element = (mass of element in
              1 mol/molar mass) * 100

# Example: water
  H: 2(1.008)/18.02 = 11.2%
  O: 16.00/18.02 = 88.8%
The percentages must add to 100.

# From experimental mass
  % = (mass of element/total mass)*100

# Uses
- Comparing fertilizers by nitrogen
content
- Checking purity
- The first step toward an empirical
formula

# Percent water in a hydrate
  % = (mass H2O/mass hydrate) * 100
Found by heating until the mass stops
changing.

@ARTICLE Empirical and Molecular Formulas
# Empirical formula
The simplest whole-number ratio of
atoms. CH2O for glucose.

# Molecular formula
The actual count. C6H12O6 for glucose.
  molecular = (empirical) * n
  n = molar mass/empirical mass

# Finding the empirical formula
1. Assume 100 g, so percents become
grams.
2. Convert each to moles.
3. Divide every result by the smallest.
4. If a value is near .5, .33 or .25,
multiply all of them by 2, 3 or 4.
5. Round to whole numbers.

# Example
40.0% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O
  C: 40.0/12.01 = 3.33
  H: 6.7/1.008  = 6.65
  O: 53.3/16.00 = 3.33
Divide by 3.33: 1, 2, 1 -> CH2O

# Then the molecular formula
If the molar mass is 180 g/mol:
  180/30.03 = 6, so C6H12O6

# Combustion analysis
All the carbon ends up in CO2 and all
the hydrogen in H2O. Work back to moles
of C and H, then get oxygen by
subtracting from the sample mass.
