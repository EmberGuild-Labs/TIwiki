@CATEGORY Acids and Bases

@ARTICLE Definitions
# Arrhenius
Acid produces H+ in water; base produces
OH-. Narrow, but simple.

# Bronsted-Lowry
Acid is a proton DONOR; base is a proton
ACCEPTOR. Works without water.
  HCl + H2O -> H3O+ + Cl-
  acid  base   acid   base

# Conjugate pairs
Differ by exactly one H+.
  HCl/Cl-       H2O/OH-
  H3O+/H2O      NH4+/NH3
A strong acid has a very weak conjugate
base.

# Lewis
Acid is an electron pair ACCEPTOR; base
is an electron pair DONOR. The broadest
definition, and it covers reactions with
no protons at all, such as BF3 with NH3.

# Amphoteric
Can act as either. Water is the classic
case, as are HCO3- and HSO4-.

# Properties
Acids taste sour, turn litmus red, react
with metals to give H2, and react with
carbonates to give CO2. Bases taste
bitter, feel slippery, and turn litmus
blue.

@ARTICLE Strong and Weak
# The strong acids (memorize)
  HCl   hydrochloric
  HBr   hydrobromic
  HI    hydroiodic
  HNO3  nitric
  H2SO4 sulfuric
  HClO4 perchloric
  HClO3 chloric
Everything else is weak.

# The strong bases
Group 1 hydroxides: LiOH, NaOH, KOH,
RbOH, CsOH.
Heavy group 2 hydroxides: Ca(OH)2,
Sr(OH)2, Ba(OH)2.

# Strong means fully ionized
A strong acid dissociates 100%, so
  0.10 M HCl has [H+] = 0.10 M
Use a single arrow.

# Weak means partial
A weak acid reaches an equilibrium, so
  0.10 M CH3COOH has [H+] << 0.10 M
Use a double arrow and Ka.

# Strong vs concentrated
Different words. Concentrated describes
how much is dissolved; strong describes
how completely it ionizes. Concentrated
acetic acid is still weak.

# Common weak acids
  HF, CH3COOH (acetic), H2CO3, H3PO4,
  HNO2, HCN
Common weak base: NH3.

@ARTICLE pH and pOH
  pH = -log[H+]
  pOH = -log[OH-]
  pH + pOH = 14   (at 25 C)

# Reverse
  [H+] = 10^(-pH)
  [OH-] = 10^(-pOH)

# Water autoionization
  Kw = [H+][OH-] = 1.0e-14 at 25 C

# The scale
  pH < 7  acidic
  pH = 7  neutral (at 25 C)
  pH > 7  basic
Each pH unit is a factor of 10 in [H+],
so pH 3 is 100 times more acidic than
pH 5.

# Worked example
0.010 M HCl is strong, so
[H+] = 0.010 = 1.0e-2 and pH = 2.00.

# Sig figs in logs
Only the digits after the decimal point
count. [H+] = 1.0e-2 with 2 sig figs
gives pH = 2.00.

# On the TI-84
Use LOG for pH and 10^x (2nd LOG) to go
back. Watch the negative sign key: use
(-), not the subtraction key.

@ARTICLE Ka, Kb and Weak Equilibria
# Acid dissociation
  HA <-> H+ + A-
  Ka = [H+][A-]/[HA]
Larger Ka means a stronger acid.

# Base ionization
  B + H2O <-> BH+ + OH-
  Kb = [BH+][OH-]/[B]

# The relationship
  Ka * Kb = Kw = 1.0e-14
for a conjugate pair. A strong acid
gives a negligibly weak conjugate base.

# pKa
  pKa = -log(Ka)
Lower pKa means a stronger acid.

# Finding pH of a weak acid
Set up an ICE table:
  Ka = x^2/([HA]0 - x)
Assume x is small compared with [HA]0:
  x = sqrt(Ka * [HA]0)
Then pH = -log(x). Check that x is under
5% of [HA]0; if not, solve the quadratic.

# Percent ionization
  % = (x/[HA]0) * 100
It rises as the solution gets more
dilute.

# Salt hydrolysis
- Salt of strong acid + strong base:
neutral (NaCl)
- Strong acid + weak base: acidic
(NH4Cl)
- Weak acid + strong base: basic
(NaC2H3O2)

@ARTICLE Titration and Buffers
# Neutralization
  acid + base -> salt + water
  H+ + OH- -> H2O   (net ionic)

# Titration calculation
  M_a*V_a*n_a = M_b*V_b*n_b
n is the protons or hydroxides per
formula unit.

# Titration curves
- Strong acid with strong base:
equivalence at pH 7, a very steep jump.
- Weak acid with strong base:
equivalence ABOVE 7, because the
conjugate base is basic.
- Weak base with strong acid:
equivalence BELOW 7.

# Half-equivalence point
Half the acid is neutralized, so
  pH = pKa
This is the fastest way to read a pKa
off a curve, and it is the center of the
buffer region.

# Indicators
Choose one whose color change spans the
equivalence pH.
  methyl orange  3.1 - 4.4
  litmus         5.0 - 8.0
  phenolphthalein 8.3 - 10.0
  (colorless to pink)

# Buffers
A weak acid with its conjugate base (or
a weak base with its conjugate acid).
They resist pH change because one
component neutralizes added acid and the
other neutralizes added base.

# Henderson-Hasselbalch
  pH = pKa + log([base]/[acid])
The buffer is most effective when the
two concentrations are equal, so pH is
pKa, and it works well within about one
pH unit of pKa.

# Buffer capacity
The amount of acid or base a buffer can
absorb. It is exhausted once one
component runs out.
