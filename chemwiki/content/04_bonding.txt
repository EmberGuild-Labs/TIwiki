@CATEGORY Chemical Bonding

@ARTICLE Ionic Bonding
Metal plus nonmetal. Electrons are
TRANSFERRED, making cations and anions
that attract electrostatically.

# Formation
  Na loses 1 e- -> Na+
  Cl gains 1 e- -> Cl-
  attraction gives NaCl

# Properties of ionic compounds
- High melting and boiling points
- Hard and brittle: shifting the lattice
puts like charges together and it
shatters
- Conduct electricity when molten or
dissolved, NOT as a solid
- Usually soluble in water
- Form crystal lattices, not molecules

# Formula units
There is no such thing as an NaCl
molecule. The formula gives the simplest
whole-number ratio in the lattice.

# Lattice energy
Energy released forming the lattice from
gaseous ions. It grows with larger
charges and smaller ions, so MgO melts
far higher than NaCl.

# Predicting formulas
Balance the charges with the crossover
rule, then reduce:
  Al3+ and O2- -> Al2O3

@ARTICLE Covalent Bonding
Nonmetal plus nonmetal. Electrons are
SHARED.

# Types
  single bond   1 shared pair
  double bond   2 shared pairs
  triple bond   3 shared pairs
More bonds means shorter and stronger.

# Bond length and strength
  C-C > C=C > C(triple)C in length
  and the reverse in strength

# Properties of molecular compounds
- Low melting and boiling points
- Often gases or liquids at room
temperature
- Do not conduct electricity
- Soluble in nonpolar solvents if
nonpolar themselves

# Polar vs nonpolar bonds
From the electronegativity difference:
  0 to 0.4     nonpolar covalent
  0.4 to 1.7   polar covalent
  above 1.7    mostly ionic
These cutoffs are guidelines, not laws.

# Network covalent
Diamond, quartz and silicon carbide are
giant covalent lattices: extremely hard
with very high melting points.

@ARTICLE Lewis Structures
# Steps
1. Count total valence electrons. Add
one per negative charge, subtract one
per positive charge.
2. Put the least electronegative atom in
the center. Never hydrogen.
3. Connect with single bonds.
4. Complete octets on the outer atoms.
5. Put leftovers on the central atom.
6. If the center is short, make double
or triple bonds using outer lone pairs.

# The octet rule
Most atoms want 8 valence electrons.
Hydrogen wants 2.

# Exceptions
- H and He: 2 electrons
- Be: often 4, B and Al: often 6
- Period 3 and beyond can expand past 8
(PCl5, SF6) using d orbitals
- Odd electron counts (NO, NO2) cannot
satisfy every octet

# Formal charge
  FC = valence - lone - (bonds)
The best structure keeps formal charges
near zero, and any negative charge on
the most electronegative atom.

# Resonance
When several equivalent structures are
possible (ozone, nitrate, benzene), the
real molecule is an average of them, not
a flicker between them.

@ARTICLE VSEPR and Molecular Shapes
Electron groups repel and spread as far
apart as possible. Count bonding groups
(a double bond counts once) plus lone
pairs on the central atom.

# 2 groups: linear, 180
  0 lone: linear (CO2)

# 3 groups: trigonal planar, 120
  0 lone: trigonal planar (BF3)
  1 lone: bent, about 118 (SO2)

# 4 groups: tetrahedral, 109.5
  0 lone: tetrahedral (CH4)
  1 lone: trigonal pyramidal (NH3, 107)
  2 lone: bent (H2O, 104.5)

# 5 groups: trig. bipyramidal, 90/120
  0 lone: trigonal bipyramidal (PCl5)
  1 lone: seesaw
  2 lone: T-shaped
  3 lone: linear

# 6 groups: octahedral, 90
  0 lone: octahedral (SF6)
  1 lone: square pyramidal
  2 lone: square planar

# Lone pairs squeeze
Lone pairs take more room than bonding
pairs, so each one shrinks the bond
angles by roughly 2 degrees.

@ARTICLE Polarity
A molecule is polar if it has polar
bonds AND they do not cancel.

# Two-step test
1. Are the bonds polar? Check the
electronegativity differences.
2. Do the dipoles cancel by symmetry?

# Symmetric, so nonpolar
  CO2 (linear)
  CH4, CCl4 (tetrahedral)
  BF3 (trigonal planar)
  SF6 (octahedral)
Identical outer atoms arranged
symmetrically always cancel.

# Asymmetric, so polar
  H2O (bent)
  NH3 (trigonal pyramidal)
  CHCl3 (different outer atoms)
A lone pair on the central atom almost
always makes a molecule polar.

# Dipole moment
Points from the positive toward the
negative end. Measured in debyes.

# Why it matters
Like dissolves like. Polar solvents
dissolve polar and ionic solutes;
nonpolar solvents dissolve nonpolar
solutes.

@ARTICLE Intermolecular Forces
Forces BETWEEN molecules, far weaker
than the bonds inside them. They set
melting and boiling points.

# Weakest to strongest
1. London dispersion forces
2. Dipole-dipole
3. Hydrogen bonding
4. Ion-dipole

# London dispersion
Present in everything. Caused by
momentary uneven electron distribution.
Stronger for larger, heavier, more
polarizable molecules, which is why
boiling point rises down the halogens.

# Dipole-dipole
Between permanent dipoles in polar
molecules.

# Hydrogen bonding
A special strong dipole-dipole when H is
bonded to N, O or F and attracted to a
lone pair on another N, O or F.
Responsible for water's high boiling
point, ice floating, and the shape of
DNA and proteins.

# Ion-dipole
Between an ion and a polar molecule.
This is what dissolves salt in water.

# Consequences
Stronger intermolecular forces mean
higher boiling point, higher viscosity,
higher surface tension and lower vapor
pressure.

@ARTICLE Metallic Bonding
A lattice of cations in a "sea" of
delocalized valence electrons that
belong to the whole crystal.

# Explains the properties
- Conducts electricity: the electrons
move freely, as a solid AND as a liquid
- Conducts heat: mobile electrons carry
energy
- Malleable and ductile: layers slide
without breaking bonds, unlike brittle
ionic solids
- Shiny: free electrons re-emit light

# Strength
More valence electrons and smaller
cations give stronger bonding and higher
melting points. Sodium melts at 98 C,
tungsten at 3422 C.

# Alloys
Solid solutions of metals. Different
sized atoms disrupt the sliding layers,
so alloys are usually harder than the
pure metals. Steel is iron plus carbon;
brass is copper plus zinc.
